## Where Averion Compass can earn its place

A practical look at property software, customer review signals, and a focused opening for Compass—grounded in public evidence and the product claims currently approved for use.

Prepared September 25, 2026United States · Multifamily operationsResearch brief · Internal working document

### Executive summary

**Recommended wedge:** help lean property teams carry an inspection finding through assigned repair, reinspection, and verified unit readiness—with clear ownership and evidence at each step.

**The opening is a workflow, not a feature checklist.** Large property platforms already advertise inspections, work orders, maintenance, and turns. Compass needs to prove it makes a specific handoff easier for a particular operator, rather than claiming to be another all-in-one property system.

Opportunity

#### Inspection-to-ready follow-through

Find teams where findings still move between a form, spreadsheet, work order queue, messages, and a final “is it ready?” check. Learn where ownership, evidence, or status gets lost.

First audience to test

#### Lean multifamily teams

Interview affordable or mixed-income operators and conventional operators with roughly 50–500 units. This range is a research hypothesis, not a proven ideal customer profile.

Credibility

#### Be specific and transparent

Averion Software LLC is a Virginia company. Compass is its property operations product. The company is newly formed; do not imply years of experience, adoption, or results that have not been documented.

Risk

#### Do not promise compliance

NSPIRE creates relevant customer discovery, but no evidence reviewed establishes that Compass supports NSPIRE workflows, validates compliance, or submits information to HUD.

#### What “bully our way in” should mean

Compete with focus, speed, direct customer contact, and a dependable pilot. Show the workflow in a live demo; ask prospects to challenge it; fix only the problems that repeat. Win on the work a customer can see. Don’t make claims about competitors that their evidence cannot support.

### The market opening

#### The job that may be underserved

Property teams have to turn observations into completed work: capture the condition, decide what happens next, assign responsibility, follow progress, confirm the fix, and decide whether the unit is ready. Every transition creates a chance for a note, photo, deadline, or responsibility to disappear. The product hypothesis is that smaller teams feel this coordination tax more acutely because they have less process support and less appetite for a large suite implementation.

Compass’s current approved message already covers inspections, recording findings, assigning follow-up work, and tracking a unit through review and sign-off. That is a credible foundation for testing the handoff. It is not yet evidence of task automation, resident messaging, audit-grade history, mobile/offline capture, PMS integrations, or regulatory reporting.

#### Affordable and mixed-income housing: a timely discovery lane

HUD says Public Housing and Multifamily Housing properties subject to an assistance contract must self-inspect all units at least annually. HUD’s current notices list October 1, 2026 as the date for scoring new affirmative requirements for Public Housing and Multifamily; HCV, PBV, and Section 8 Moderate Rehabilitation compliance was extended through January 31, 2027, with February 1, 2027 shown as the applicable date. This gives operators a reason to examine inspection and correction processes now.

It does **not** prove there is unserved demand for a separate tool. HUD programs have different rules, and affordable housing software vendors already sell compliance and property operations suites. Treat NSPIRE as a customer-research prompt, not a product claim or a wedge by itself.

**Position carefully:** “We’re exploring how property teams keep inspection follow-up and repair evidence moving.” Avoid “NSPIRE compliant,” “HUD approved,” “automated compliance,” or “ready for HUD reporting” unless the product, requirements, and legal language have all been validated.

#### Three opportunity hypotheses to test

| Hypothesis | Why it may matter | What would disprove it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Follow-through is the gap.** Teams can inspect, but repairing and confirming findings is scattered. | Review and product evidence point to inspection, work, and reporting workflows being split or customized. | Operators already have a simple, reliable flow in their PMS and see no cost in switching or adding a tool. |
| **Lean teams want a focused layer.** Some operators value a smaller tool that handles one operational sequence clearly. | Broad suites may be costly or heavy for smaller teams; AppFolio currently publishes a 50-unit minimum. | Buyers insist on a single system of record, and integrations make a standalone layer unacceptable. |
| **Evidence improves handoffs.** Photos, statuses, ownership, and completion confirmation reduce repeated checking. | Inspection reviews mention photos, checklists, and durable records; the operational need is plausible. | Evidence capture adds staff burden without changing closeout time or rework. |

#### Recurring review themes worth probing

##### Records and evidence

Can a team retrieve what was seen, when it was seen, and what happened next? Is the photo attached to the condition or buried in a separate message?

##### Handoffs between teams

Where does an inspection finding become a maintenance task? Who owns it after assignment? How do supervisors know it is actually done?

##### Workflow fit and configuration

Which inspection and turn variations require repeated setup? Does flexibility help, or does it leave the process hard to teach and maintain?

##### Mobile field work

Can staff capture information reliably while walking units? Which device, connectivity, permissions, and saving behaviors matter in real buildings?

Review evidence is intentionally paraphrased. Always inspect the linked review page for full context and date before quoting a reviewer or using a claim publicly.

### Competitive reality

This is an active category. A viable story must be more precise than “we do inspections and turns.”

| Competitor | What it already markets | Strategic consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **AppFolio** | Its public pricing includes maintenance, inspections, and unit turns; the company describes offline inspection capture, notes/photos, follow-ups, and work-order creation. Its 2025 product update describes turn automation, inspection scheduling, and notifications. Published pricing specifies a 50-unit minimum. | Generic turns, inspections, offline use, and basic follow-up are not open positioning territory. Find an underserved buyer or prove a markedly easier flow. |
| **Buildium** | Broad property management platform with centralized leasing, maintenance, resident communication, and accounting capabilities reflected in product messaging and positive reviews. | Complementary workflow may be easier to sell than replacing an established property system. |
| **Yardi / MRI** | Affordable-housing products advertise compliance and broad property management. Yardi materials also include inspections, tasks, and maintenance functions. | Affordable housing is an attractive discovery segment, but not a competitor-free niche. Focus on physical follow-through only if operator interviews expose a gap. |
| **HappyCo / specialist tools** | Purpose-built property operations offerings market inspections, maintenance, and unit turns. NSPIRE-specific tools such as NSPIRE AID also promote inspection and deficiency workflows. | “Specialized” and “NSPIRE app” are insufficient differentiators. Confirm the underserved workflow, integration expectations, and what teams will pay for. |

#### Likely reasons buyers stay with incumbents

- One vendor already connects accounting, leasing, resident records, maintenance, and reporting.

- Existing data and staff habits are expensive to move.

- Buyers may prefer a module or integration to another login and another source of truth.

- Incumbents can add similar features; workflow claims are easy to copy without customer proof and distribution.

#### Where a small entrant can compete

- Show one workflow end-to-end in a few minutes, using the customer's actual process and language.

- Offer direct access to the people improving the product and a scoped, measured pilot.

- Target an operator whose problem is frequent, visible, and owned by someone who can buy.

- Document specific customer-approved evidence as soon as a pilot delivers it.

### Positioning and About Us

#### Positioning to test

**For lean property teams that need to move from inspections to ready units, Averion Compass helps staff record findings, assign follow-up work, and track a unit through review and sign-off—so the next step is easier to see.**

Keep the promise anchored in what is currently stated on the website and positioning notes. As capabilities are confirmed, add details about evidence, work order handoff, mobile use, integrations, or compliance one at a time.

#### About Us copy: confidence through candor

We build software around the work people need to get done.

Averion Software LLC is a Virginia company developing products for distinct needs. Averion Compass helps property teams record inspection findings, assign follow-up work, and see what remains before a unit is ready. StellaAI is a separate product for automated crypto trading.

We believe good software should make work easier to understand and leave people in control. We keep improving our products as we learn what works and what needs to change.

Have a workflow you’d like to talk through? Get in touch.

This wording does not suggest a founding story, team credentials, customer count, years in business, or proven outcomes. Add those only when there is evidence and the people involved approve the details.

#### Product story in one breath

**Averion Compass helps property teams move inspection findings through follow-up and review, so staff can see what still needs attention before a unit is ready.**

Keep StellaAI separate in navigation and company descriptions. It serves a different buyer and should not be used as evidence of Compass’s property-management experience.

### How to enter the market

#### Beachhead experiment

| Element | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Target | Operations leaders and property managers at affordable/mixed-income and conventional multifamily operators, beginning with lean teams. Test roughly 50–500 units as a screening range. |
| Problem | Inspection observations are not consistently assigned, followed, verified, and reflected in the unit-ready decision. |
| Message | “From inspection finding to verified fix.” Explain the actual steps in the demo; avoid broad claims about saving time until measured. |
| Offer | A tightly scoped design-partner pilot around one recurring workflow, with baseline and end-of-pilot measures. Use the approved discounted pilot/optional price-lock structure only after scope and commercial terms are approved. |
| Channel | Founder-led conversations, warm introductions, local property-management associations, affordable-housing operator networks, and direct outreach based on public company-level fit. Keep outreach specific and respectful. |
| Action | Recruit 8–12 discovery conversations across two segments; invite the strongest-fit teams into a demo and then a 2–3 property pilot if capabilities and data handling fit. |
| Primary metric | Share of interviewed teams that describe the same handoff problem, identify a budget owner, and agree to test a measurable workflow. |
| Owner / checkpoint | Company leadership; review interview notes weekly and choose a segment after the first 8 interviews. |

#### Discovery questions

- Walk me through your last unit inspection that uncovered work. What happened after the inspector left?

- Where do findings, photos, assignments, deadlines, and completion evidence live today?

- Which handoff creates the most calls, messages, re-entry, or rework?

- How do you decide and record that a unit is ready? Who can make that call?

- How many inspections/turns happen in a typical month, and where does the process vary by property or program?

- Which system must remain the source of truth? What would a new tool need to connect to or export?

- What does a missed or delayed handoff cost in staff time, vacancy days, resident experience, or compliance exposure?

- Who would sponsor and pay for a pilot? What would have to be true to keep using it?

### Proof plan: earn the claims

#### Measure before and after

| Measure | Definition to agree with pilot team | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finding-to-assignment time | Elapsed time from finding captured to a person or team accepting ownership. | Tests whether the handoff is easier to act on. |
| Open finding age | Time findings remain unresolved, segmented by priority/type and property. | Shows whether work stalls; avoid claiming causation from small samples. |
| Verified completion time | Elapsed time from initial finding to repair completion confirmed by the team’s agreed evidence. | Tests the complete loop rather than data entry alone. |
| Reinspection close rate | Share of items that pass the next check without another repair cycle. | Signals rework, but needs consistent criteria and enough cases. |
| Days to ready | From inspection or vacancy start to documented ready decision. | Connects execution to an operator outcome; record other causes of delay. |
| Staff touches and evidence completeness | Manual follow-ups/re-entry per finding; share with required photo/notes attached. | Tests admin burden and whether evidence is consistently available. |

#### Product capability checks before a pilot promise

- Validate who can create, assign, update, verify, and close a finding in the current release.

- Confirm photo and document handling, timestamps, edit history, export, retention, permissions, and backups.

- Test mobile performance on customer devices and connectivity conditions; don’t claim offline capability unless verified.

- Map the pilot workflow to each property’s current system and agree which system remains authoritative.

- For affordable housing, have a knowledgeable program/compliance reviewer validate any checklist content. Keep Compass framed as workflow support unless formal compliance support is established.

- Obtain customer approval before using a name, logo, quote, screenshots, or outcome publicly.

#### Thirty-day action sequence

| Timing | Work | Decision output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Days 1–7 | Complete 8–12 interviews split across affordable/mixed-income and conventional multifamily. Capture current tools, events, costs, buying authority, and the exact handoff language. | Does one segment repeat the problem and have a reachable buyer? |
| Days 8–14 | Map the current Compass workflow against the strongest recurring journey. Run live demos and record every missing capability or integration need. | Can the released product support the core journey without custom development? |
| Days 15–21 | Agree a pilot scope with a suitable design partner: properties, roles, workflow, duration, data boundaries, baseline, and success definitions. | Is there a safe, measurable pilot with an owner and a path to purchase? |
| Days 22–30 | Begin the pilot if product readiness and commercial terms are approved. Review results weekly and log issues, adoption, and operator quotes. | Continue, adjust scope, or stop based on usage and the baseline. |

- Review findings are examples and qualitative signals. No statistical review coding or representative sample was conducted.

- Market segment sizes and 50–500 unit range are hypotheses for discovery, not validated TAM or ICP estimates.
